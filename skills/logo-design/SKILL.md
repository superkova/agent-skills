# Logo Design

Create a mark whose meaning, construction, and practical behavior support the brand. Adapt the depth to the request: a critique needs evidence and prioritized fixes; a new identity needs concept exploration and usable assets. Preserve an established mark when the task is a refinement.

The workflow draws on Satori Graphics' logo-design tutorial. Read [the timestamped source notes](references/video-notes.md) when explaining its provenance or revisiting a particular lesson. They are paraphrased notes, not a transcript. The implementation and delivery instructions below adapt those ideas for agent-assisted work.

## Establish the design problem

Use the supplied brief, existing identity, and relevant project files before asking questions. Establish the exact brand name, audience, offer, difference from alternatives, desired impression, and the places the logo must work. Ask only for missing details that materially change the design; label assumptions for exploratory work.

Turn broad adjectives into observable requirements. For example, a restrained clothing brand might require an uncluttered silhouette, readable small labels, and a recognizable avatar. Keep audience meaning and functional constraints separate so a readable but inappropriate concept does not pass automatically.

When the tone is unclear, use language to expose it: identify words the brand would avoid, words it would naturally use, and a short introduction in its voice. Treat these as hypotheses to inform form, weight, spacing, and motion, not universal rules about shape psychology.

## Start in the intended setting

Before detailed drawing, put the name in plain type into representative placements at realistic sizes. Include the smallest important use. Choose contexts from the brief—such as a mobile header, social avatar, garment label, or product surface—rather than generic decorative mockups.

Inspect relevant competitors when research is in scope. Compare marks at similar perceived sizes on the same background. Identify recurring silhouettes, letterforms, proportions, and conventions. Decide which conventions aid recognition and where the brand can differ. Do not invent competitor findings when access is unavailable.

## Explore different ideas

Explore concepts from three starting points: the offering itself, the way it is delivered, and the benefit or feeling it creates. Produce meaningfully different directions rather than cosmetic variations on one obvious industry symbol.

Consider lettering and letter relationships alongside symbols. A wordmark may solve the brief without a separate icon. Describe each direction's central idea in a sentence; simplify concepts that depend on a long explanation or several competing metaphors.

For a promising direction, test whether one characteristic can support a useful application beyond the mark, such as a crop, layout accent, or reveal. Let that experiment inform the design without turning a logo request into a complete identity project.

## Compare with evidence

Show alternatives under equivalent conditions: start with a plain monochrome presentation, then place them in the same relevant application at its real size. Avoid giving one concept a more flattering mockup.

Evaluate each against the brief:

- Does it suggest the intended character and central idea?
- Is its silhouette or lettering distinguishable from the relevant alternatives?
- Does it remain clear at the smallest required placement without zooming?
- Does it work in the required crop, contrast, and production conditions?

State the observed strength, weakness, and resulting revision or recommendation. Do not treat personal preference as a test result.

When audience feedback is available, show the mark without a rationale and collect spontaneous impressions. A brief viewing followed by a delayed lineup can expose confusion with other marks. Record what people actually did or said. Without participants, provide the test procedure and mark perception and recall as untested; never simulate human validation as evidence.

## Refine and build only useful variants

Inspect vector paths in outline view. Remove redundant points, resolve unintended kinks or flat spots, and smooth transitions while preserving deliberate corners and character. Use as few points as the intended shape needs; do not minimize points mechanically. Check spacing and optical balance in the rendered result as well as the geometry.

Assign each variant a concrete placement. Compare a full name and compact mark inside the same actual-size avatar before deciding whether both are needed. Add reverse, stacked, or simplified variants only when a required use supports them.

If motion is requested, derive its direction and timing from the mark's structure and brand tone. Judge the static mark independently. Do not add animation merely to make a presentation impressive.

Use the user's chosen tools where practical. For new raster concept exploration, use an available image-generation capability when useful; for an existing SVG or vector identity, work directly in its native geometry. Deliver editable vector artwork when final production assets are requested and supported. A bitmap embedded in an SVG is not a vector reconstruction. Label raster concepts and any unfinished production work clearly.

## Verify and hand over

Open or render the actual exported files and inspect the required placements, including the smallest use and relevant light/dark backgrounds. Check for clipping, missing fonts, unexpected transparency, or changed geometry. Report only checks actually performed.

For production delivery, name files by brand, variant, color treatment, and format. Provide a short mapping from intended use to file: website, avatar, print, or other requested placement. Keep editable source and distribution exports distinguishable. Identify any physical process that still needs a sample, and avoid claiming a format is printer-ready without the relevant specifications.

Match the final response to the scope: show the concepts or finished assets, explain the recommendation using the brief, link usable files, and identify material untested requirements. Do not make every critique produce a full asset package.
